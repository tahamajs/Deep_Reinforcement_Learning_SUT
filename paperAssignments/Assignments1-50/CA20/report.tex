\FOR{each epoch}

  \ENDFOR

\section{Background}
\section{Motivation}
Many real-world applications of reinforcement learning (RL) require not only maximizing performance but also adhering to safety, fairness, or resource constraints. For example, in robotics, an agent must avoid unsafe states; in finance, risk must be controlled; in healthcare, treatment policies must satisfy ethical and regulatory requirements. Standard RL algorithms do not account for such constraints, which can lead to unsafe or impractical solutions. This motivates the study of constrained RL, where the agent must optimize its policy while satisfying one or more constraints. The Lagrangian approach provides a principled and flexible framework for handling constraints, making it a popular choice in both research and practice.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a framework for sequential decision making, where an agent interacts with an environment to maximize cumulative reward. In standard RL, the agent learns a policy $\pi(a|s)$ that maps states to actions, aiming to maximize expected return. However, in many real-world applications, agents must also satisfy constraints, such as safety, resource limits, or fairness. Constrained RL extends the standard framework by introducing additional cost signals and requiring that expected constraint costs remain below a threshold. Lagrangian relaxation is a classic approach to handle such constraints, introducing a multiplier to penalize violations in the objective.

Policy gradient methods~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,schulman2015trust} are a family of RL algorithms that directly optimize the policy parameters via gradient ascent on expected return. When constraints are present, the Lagrangian method augments the loss with a penalty term, and the multiplier is updated by projected gradient ascent. This approach is simple, general, and forms the basis for many safe RL algorithms.

\section{Design Choices}
This project uses a synthetic bandit-style dataset for rapid prototyping and debugging. The policy and value networks are implemented as simple multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), which are sufficient for low-dimensional problems and allow for fast iteration. The Lagrangian approach is chosen for its simplicity and extensibility: it can be applied to a wide range of constraints and is compatible with most policy optimization algorithms. The code is structured to be import-safe, modular, and easy to extend to more complex environments or policies.

\section{Introduction}
Constrained reinforcement learning (RL) arises when a learning agent must optimize task performance subject to constraints (safety, resource usage, fairness, etc.). A common approach is to form a Lagrangian relaxation of the constrained optimization problem and perform joint updates of policy parameters and a non-negative Lagrange multiplier. This simple approach is widely used in practice and acts as a good pedagogical introduction to constrained RL.

This document accompanies a compact code scaffold (CA20) intended for short reproducible experiments. We focus on clarity, import-safety, and minimal dependencies so results are easy to reproduce and extend.

\section{Related work}
Classic references include the standard policy-gradient literature~\cite{sutton2018reinforcement,schulman2015trust} and constrained policy optimization methods such as Constrained Policy Optimization (CPO) and related approaches that use Lagrangian relaxation and multiplier updates~\cite{achiam2017constrained}. Our implementation targets the simplest working pipeline for classroom experiments and comparisons.

\section{Problem formulation}
\subsection{Formal Definition}
Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the state space, $\mathcal{A}$ the action space, $P(s'|s,a)$ the transition dynamics, $r(s,a)$ the reward function, and $c(s,a)$ the constraint cost. The agent's objective is to find a policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)$ that maximizes expected return while ensuring the expected constraint cost does not exceed a threshold $c$:
\begin{align}
  \max_{\theta} \; & \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^T \gamma^t r(s_t,a_t)\right] \\
  	ext{subject to} \; & \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t=0}^T \gamma^t c(s_t,a_t)\right] \leq c
\end{align}
where $\tau$ denotes a trajectory $(s_0,a_0,s_1,...)$ and $\gamma$ is the discount factor.

We consider a (finite-horizon or episodic) MDP with return $R(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r_t$ and an additional per-trajectory constraint cost $C(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^T c_t$. The constrained optimization problem is
\begin{equation}
  \max_{\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)] \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[C(\tau)] \le c.
\end{equation}
We form the Lagrangian
\begin{equation}
  L(\theta,\mu) = -\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)] + \mu\left(\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[C(\tau)] - c\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mu \ge 0$ is the Lagrange multiplier and the sign convention places reward minimization in the loss (consistent with many PyTorch trainings).

\section{Method and updates}
\subsection{Theoretical Analysis}
The Lagrangian relaxation transforms the constrained problem into a saddle-point optimization. Under mild regularity conditions, strong duality holds, and the optimal policy and multiplier can be found by alternating gradient steps. The policy update seeks to maximize the Lagrangian, while the multiplier update enforces the constraint. In practice, stochastic gradient estimates and finite samples introduce noise, so careful tuning of learning rates and regularization is required. The method is general and can be extended to multiple constraints by introducing a vector of multipliers.

We use a Gaussian MLP policy $\pi_\theta(a|s)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$ (diagonal, state-independent log std) and a value network $V_\phi(s)$ as a baseline. At each training batch we compute per-sample rewards and constraints, estimate advantages $\hat{A}=r - V_\phi(s)$ and compute the policy gradient loss:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_\text{policy} = -\frac{1}{B}\sum_i \log\pi_\theta(a_i|s_i) \hat{A}_i.
\end{equation}
The scalar batch constraint estimate is $\hat{C}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_i c_i$. We combine terms into a Lagrangian loss optimized w.r.t. $\theta$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_\text{policy} + \mu \left(\hat{C} - c\right).
\end{equation}
Policy parameters are updated by standard gradient descent on $\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ (via Adam). The multiplier is updated by projected gradient ascent:
\begin{equation}
  \mu \leftarrow \max\left(0, \mu + \eta_\mu (\hat{C} - c)\right)
\end{equation}
with optional clipping for stability.

\section{Algorithm (pseudocode)}
\begin{verbatim}
for each epoch:
  for batch in dataloader:
    obs, actions, rewards, constraints = batch
    actions_pred, logp = policy.sample(obs)
    values = value(obs)
    advantages = rewards - values.detach()
    p_loss = -mean(logp * advantages)
    c_val = mean(constraints)
    lag_loss = p_loss + mu * (c_val - c)

    # update policy (lagrangian loss)
    policy_opt.zero_grad(); lag_loss.backward(retain_graph=True)
    policy_opt.step()

    # update value network
    v_loss = mse(values, rewards)
    value_opt.zero_grad(); v_loss.backward(); value_opt.step()

    # update multiplier
    mu = max(0, mu + lambda_lr * (c_val - c))
\end{verbatim}

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Societal Impact}
Safe and constrained RL has significant societal implications. In safety-critical domains, such as autonomous driving or healthcare, ensuring that learned policies respect constraints is essential for trust and adoption. However, poorly specified constraints or inadequate enforcement can lead to unintended consequences. It is important to carefully design constraints and monitor policy behavior during deployment.

The Lagrangian approach often yields a trade-off between reward and constraint adherence: tighter constraints (smaller $c$) generally reduce average return but also reduce violations. Multiplier learning rate and clipping are important knobs: too-large steps can cause oscillation in multiplier and degraded learning.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\subsection{Limitations}
While the Lagrangian approach is flexible and easy to implement, it has several limitations. The method may require careful tuning of the multiplier learning rate and clipping parameters. In high-dimensional or highly stochastic environments, constraint satisfaction may be difficult to guarantee. The approach assumes that constraint costs are observable and differentiable, which may not hold in all settings. Finally, the method does not provide explicit guarantees on worst-case constraint violations.

\subsection{Future Work}
Future research directions include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Extending the method to handle multiple, possibly conflicting, constraints.
  \item Incorporating model-based RL or planning for improved sample efficiency.
  \item Developing adaptive or meta-learning strategies for tuning multipliers.
  \item Applying the approach to real-world environments with complex safety requirements.
  \item Integrating formal verification or runtime monitoring for stronger safety guarantees.
\end{itemize}
This scaffold targets short experiments in synthetic settings. Future extensions include episodic environment evaluations, proper advantage estimators (GAE), trust-region style constrained optimization (e.g., CPO), and including logging/experiment tracking (TensorBoard/W&B).

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval}

To assess the performance of the constrained policy, we define the following metrics:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Average Return}: The mean episodic return across evaluation trajectories. This measures task performance.
  \item \textbf{Constraint Violation Rate}: The fraction of evaluation trajectories where the constraint cost exceeds the threshold $c$. This measures safety adherence.
  \item \textbf{Multiplier Stability}: The final value of the Lagrange multiplier $\mu$ after training, indicating how much penalty was applied.
  \item \textbf{Training Stability}: Variance in policy loss and constraint estimates over epochs, to detect oscillations or divergence.
\end{itemize}

For reproducibility, we recommend evaluating on a held-out synthetic dataset or a small environment (e.g., a modified CartPole with added constraints). Report metrics as mean $\pm$ std across 3-5 random seeds.

\subsection{Example Metric Computation}
In code, constraint violation rate can be computed as:
\begin{verbatim}
def compute_violation_rate(constraints, threshold):
    return (constraints > threshold).float().mean().item()
\end{verbatim}
Where \texttt{constraints} is a tensor of per-episode constraint costs.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The Lagrangian approach often yields a trade-off between reward and constraint adherence: tighter constraints (smaller $c$) generally reduce average return but also reduce violations. Multiplier learning rate and clipping are important knobs: too-large steps can cause oscillation in multiplier and degraded learning.

In our synthetic setting, constraints are correlated with observation features (e.g., obs[:,1] > 0 triggers violations), allowing the policy to learn to avoid high-constraint regions. This mimics real-world scenarios where constraints depend on state or actions.

Potential failure modes include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multiplier Explosion}: If $\eta_\mu$ is too large, $\mu$ can grow unbounded, leading to overly conservative policies.
  \item \textbf{Constraint Underestimation}: In stochastic environments, batch estimates may not reflect true expected constraints, requiring more samples or smoothing.
  \item \textbf{Gradient Conflicts}: The Lagrangian term can conflict with reward gradients, slowing convergence or causing instability.
\end{itemize}

Compared to unconstrained policy gradients, the constrained version adds minimal overhead (one scalar update per batch) while providing interpretable trade-offs via the multiplier.

\section{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:limitations}

This scaffold targets short experiments in synthetic settings. Limitations include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Synthetic data may not capture real environment dynamics or constraint correlations.
  \item No proper episodic evaluation (trajectories are sampled independently).
  \item Simple advantage estimation (no GAE or bootstrapping).
  \item No exploration bonuses or entropy regularization.
\end{itemize}

Future extensions include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Integrating with Gym environments (e.g., SafetyGym) for episodic constraints.
  \item Adding trust-region methods (TRPO, PPO) for stable constrained updates.
  \item Multi-constraint scenarios with separate multipliers.
  \item Experiment tracking with Weights \& Biases or TensorBoard.
  \item Theoretical analysis of convergence guarantees.
\end{itemize}

Here we provide the complete training loop in pseudocode, including initialization and logging:

\begin{verbatim}
# Initialization
config = load_config("configs/debug.yaml")
set_seed(config.seed)
policy = MLPPolicy(config.obs_dim, config.action_dim, config.hidden_dim)
value = ValueNet(config.obs_dim, config.hidden_dim)
policy_opt = Adam(policy.parameters(), lr=config.lr)
value_opt = Adam(value.parameters(), lr=config.lr)
lagrange = LagrangeMultiplier(initial=1.0, lr=config.lambda_lr, clip=config.lambda_clip)
dataloader = make_dataloader(batch_size=config.batch_size, ...)

# Training loop
for epoch in range(config.epochs):
    epoch_policy_loss = 0.0
    epoch_value_loss = 0.0
    epoch_constraint = 0.0
    num_batches = 0
    
    for batch in dataloader:
        obs, actions, rewards, constraints = batch
        
        # Forward pass
        actions_pred, logp = policy.sample(obs)
        values = value(obs)
        advantages = rewards - values.detach()
        
        # Losses
        p_loss = policy_loss_from_logprob(logp, advantages)
        c_val = compute_constraint(constraints)
        lag_loss = lagrangian_loss(p_loss, c_val, lagrange.value, config.constraint_c)
        v_loss = mse_loss(values, rewards)
        
        # Updates
        policy_opt.zero_grad()
        lag_loss.backward(retain_graph=True)
        policy_opt.step()
        
        value_opt.zero_grad()
        v_loss.backward()
        value_opt.step()
        
        lagrange.step(float(c_val), config.constraint_c)
        
        # Accumulate metrics
        epoch_policy_loss += p_loss.item()
        epoch_value_loss += v_loss.item()
        epoch_constraint += c_val.item()
        num_batches += 1
    
    # Log epoch metrics
    print(f"Epoch {epoch}: Policy Loss {epoch_policy_loss/num_batches:.4f}, "
          f"Value Loss {epoch_value_loss/num_batches:.4f}, "
          f"Constraint {epoch_constraint/num_batches:.4f}, "
          f"Lagrange {lagrange.value:.4f}")

# Save checkpoint
save_checkpoint("outputs/ca20_checkpoint.pt", policy, policy_opt, 
                extra={"lagrange": lagrange.state_dict()})
\end{verbatim}
